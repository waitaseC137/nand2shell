# 👾 CWE-1242 — Inclusion of Undocumented Features or Chicken Bits

> **Things that work but are not in the documentation.** A piece of hardware
> carries control bits that never appear in its documentation. Most of the time
> they were left there with good intentions. But being undocumented does not mean
> **being unreachable** — and if one of them switches off a security feature,
> there is an entry point there.

## What a Chicken Bit Is

The name is odd, the story makes sense.

Once a chip has been manufactured it **cannot be changed.** In software you fix
a faulty line and publish a new version; there is no such thing in silicon.
Millions of parts have been made and the circuit inside them will stay that way
forever.

That is why, when designers bring in a risky feature, they put a **switch to turn
it off** next to it. If the feature causes trouble after manufacturing, it is
disabled with that bit and the chip is saved.

In English, *"to chicken out"* means to get scared and back down. That is exactly
this bit's job: **the back-down bit.**

> 🔑 A chicken bit is not bad design. Quite the opposite: it is the engineering
> answer to leaving a way back in an environment that cannot be fixed. The
> weakness comes not from the bit existing, but from **it being undocumented and
> staying reachable.**

## The Documented Space, the Real Space

In [lesson 14](../salterden_bilgisayara/14_alu.md) you built the ALU's control
word:

```
u  op1  op0  zx  sw     =  5 bits  →  2⁵ = 32 combinations
```

What did the documentation give you? Two separate tables:

- **8 rows** for `u`, `op1`, `op0`
- **4 rows** for `zx`, `sw` — and only through the `X − Y` example (one shared
  with the first table, three new)

The two axes are described separately; **their product is written nowhere.**
Each of the 32 combinations produces a result; the documentation covers eleven
of them.

The remaining 21 combinations do not disappear. They sit in the chip, they work,
they produce a value. They are simply **not described.**

```
the documented state space  ⊂  the real state space
                            ↑
              the difference: the place to look
```

In NandGame this difference is harmless — what comes out is just extra
operations (the count in the lesson finds **19 distinct operations**; 11 written
in the documentation, 8 unlisted). What is more, the game gives the **rule** for
`zx` and `sw`, so those eight operations can be derived on paper: not hidden,
just unlisted. On a real chicken bit not even the rule is written down, and
nobody even knows the bit exists. There the same picture means something
entirely different.

---

## Where the Problem Starts

The weakness is born when three conditions come together:

```
①  the bit is undocumented        →  nobody knows it exists
②  the bit is reachable           →  software can get to it
③  the bit switches off a defence →  whoever reaches it can run without that defence
```

If not all three are present, there is no problem. An undocumented bit that
cannot be reached from anywhere is dead circuitry. An undocumented bit that only
resets a counter is harmless too.

The danger is where all three intersect. And what makes the danger bigger is
this:

> **Being undocumented is not a defence.** The documentation is not the only
> source of access to a chip. The circuit itself is always there and can be
> examined.

In the security literature this is called *security through obscurity*, and
this is exactly why it does not work: the secret is buried inside the thing that
keeps it.

---

## In Real Life

This is not a theoretical worry. Two well-known cases:

**The undocumented coprocessor in VIA C3 processors (2018).** A security
researcher showed that some old VIA C3 processors contained a second processing
unit that never appears in the documentation, enabled by an undocumented bit.
The finding was presented openly at DEF CON, and the affected chips were an old
generation. The method was this: the instruction space the documentation
described was compared with the instruction space the chip actually accepted —
in other words, **the difference was scanned**.

**The Intel Management Engine's disable bit (2017).** Researchers found that a
configuration bit not present in the vendor's documentation disabled the ME. The
bit had probably been left for an institutional requirement; but being
undocumented created an asymmetry between those who knew it and those who did
not. The finding was published responsibly.

The shared lesson of both is the same: **the difference sat in the circuit, not
in the documentation.**

## The Count Reverse Engineering Does

This CWE's place in your catalogue is **Tier 3** — it is not needed to
understand NandGame, but you will meet it later. The reason is this: one of the
first steps of hardware reverse engineering is exactly the count you did in
lesson 14.

```
1.  How many bits is the control word?       →  the real state space = 2ⁿ
2.  How many states does the documentation describe?  →  the documented space
3.  How big is the difference?               →  the list of places to look
4.  What do the states in the difference do? →  observe and record
```

At the ALU level this count was `32 − 11`. On a real chip the control word may be
32 bits and the documentation may describe hundreds of rows — the count stays the
same, the scale changes.

> 🔑 The rule is one sentence: **every control word wider than its documented
> state space is a place to look.**

The rule holds on the defence side too. If you are auditing hardware, the first
thing you ask is not "what does the documentation say" but **"does the
documentation say everything"**.

## How to Prevent It

- **Describe the state space, do not give examples.** If the documentation
  describes the two axes separately and leaves the product empty, the gap becomes
  undocumented behaviour. The two tables in lesson 14 are a small version of
  this.
- **Define the undefined combinations.** Even the sentence "this combination is
  reserved, its behaviour is not guaranteed" is better than a gap; but best of all
  is for those combinations to produce a **fixed and harmless** result.
- **Close the debug paths in production.** If interfaces left for testing survive
  into production, they become a permanent entry point. Closing them should be
  permanent, with a *fuse*, not with a software setting.
- **Document and authorise the chicken bit.** There is nothing wrong with it
  existing; there is something wrong with it being hidden. Accessing it should
  require privilege, and using it should be logged.
- **Scan your own state space yourself.** Assume the vendor did not do the count.
  Verification tests should cover not the documented rows but **every
  combination**.
- **Do not count "nobody knows" as a defence.** The circuit can be examined by
  anyone who has it; the documentation is only a convenience.
