# ⚡ From Switches to a Computer — Interlude: The Forbidden Zone

> In Lesson 01 I told you this: **1 = there's current in the wire, 0 = there isn't.**
> This lesson opens up what's underneath that sentence. The sentence was right for
> relays; on a chip it is **incomplete**, and in one place it even turns wrong: on a
> chip a wire carries voltage, not current, and a 1 that sits still draws almost no
> current. In reality a wire doesn't have two states; it has an
> infinite number of in-between values. A computer can exist at all only because
> those in-between values are **forbidden.**

> Let me say one thing up front: nothing you learn here changes a single circuit
> you've built. What changes is that you'll know **what those circuits are standing
> on.**

> *(Interlude: no circuits, no game level. At the end there's one measurement you'll
> take on your own chip.)*

## What I Told You Was Incomplete

What a wire carries isn't current, it's **voltage** — the difference in electrical
push between two points. And it isn't a switch, it's a **dial**: it can take any
value from zero up to the supply voltage.

A modern processor core runs at roughly **0 to 1 volt**. So a wire can be sitting
at any of these:

```
0.00 V   0.13 V   0.42 V   0.58 V   0.79 V   0.96 V   ...
```

So which of those is "1"? The right answer: **none of them, by itself.** A wire
never knew it was carrying a 1 — we talked about that in Lesson 01. Now we take
one more step: the wire isn't even two-valued. **We** are the ones who decide it
will be.

---

## The Grey Band Between Is Forbidden

Here's how the decision gets made. A gate looks at the voltage on its input and
says:

```
       0 V                                              VDD
        ├──────────────┬──────────────┬──────────────────┤
        │  I READ 0    │  FORBIDDEN   │     I READ 1     │
        └──────────────┴──────────────┴──────────────────┘
             0–30%          30–70%          70–100%
```

(`VDD`: the supply voltage, the top of the range.)

If I see the bottom band, `0`. If I see the top band, `1`. **I'm not supposed to
see the middle band at all** — a signal that lands there is undefined.

At first glance this looks wasteful: we're throwing away nearly half the range.
We aren't. The forbidden band keeps 0 and 1 **apart.** A gate drives its output deep
into its band, close to the rail; a gate reading it accepts anything from the band's
edge onward. The distance between a clean output and that edge is called the
**noise margin.**

Because the voltage on a real wire never sits still:

- When the wire next to it changes value, it couples voltage onto this one
- The supply sags a little under a sudden load
- When the temperature changes, transistor behaviour drifts
- A long wire collects ambient noise like an antenna

The signal is constantly trembling. The noise margin is that trembling's
**budget:** a cleanly driven 1 that strays by `0.1 V` is still inside the top band
and still reads correctly. The forbidden band marks where the budget runs out: a
signal that lands there belongs to no band at all.

> 🔑 The forbidden zone isn't a flaw, it's **the design itself.** Eliminating noise
> is impossible; what we do instead is give noise a room to move around in, and
> treat spilling out of that room as the error.

---

## Every Gate Cleans the Signal

Now the real question. Say a gate's output is `0.92 V` — inside the top band, but
not the full `1.00 V`. That signal feeds the next gate, which degrades it a
little more, then the next one... after a thousand gates, what's left?

In an analogue chain the answer is bleak: **mud.** Every stage adds its own noise,
the degradation accumulates, the signal disappears. That's why music copied from
cassette to cassette gets a little worse with every copy.

In a digital chain that doesn't happen. Here's why:

> **A gate takes its input "a bit dirty" but puts out something SPOTLESS** — as long
> as the input stays inside its band.

The gate sees `0.92 V` on the input, says "that's the top band, so it's a 1," and
drives its output to a full `1.00 V`. The `0.08 V` of degradation at the input
**does not pass through** — it dies at the gate. This is called signal
**restoration.** If the input has fallen into the forbidden band, there is no such
promise: what the gate will drive is not defined. That is why the band is forbidden.

```
 input        gate          output       gate         output
0.92 V  ───►  [ = 1 ]  ───► 1.00 V ───► [ = 1 ]  ───► 1.00 V
 dirty        cleans it     clean       cleans it     clean
```

Building a computer out of billions of gates **rests on this:** error doesn't
accumulate, because every gate erases the accumulated error.

## Why Exactly Two Levels?

If we're dividing the range into bands anyway, why two? We could have made it
three:

```
two levels:  ├────0────┤      FORBIDDEN      ├────1────┤    ← wide margin
three:       ├──0──┤ FORB. ├──1──┤ FORB. ├──2──┤            ← every margin is narrow
```

Squeeze three bands into the same voltage range and the share going to each band
and each forbidden zone shrinks. The noise stays the same, so your tolerance is
cut in half. At four levels it shrinks further.

So binary won not because it's the "correct" system, but because it gives you
**the widest safety margin per volt.**

Don't assume ternary was never tried. **Setun** (Moscow State University, 1958) was
a working ternary computer; 50 were built between 1959 and 1965. The *balanced
ternary* system it used was mathematically elegant — it carried negative numbers
naturally, with no separate sign needed.

But Setun doesn't answer this lesson's question, "should one wire carry three
levels?": it kept each ternary digit not as three voltages on one wire but in **two
magnetic cores.** It built ternary numbers on top of two-state parts. And its
production didn't end in a technical defeat: the factory found the sale price too
low and stopped making it, and the binary machine that replaced it cost 2.5 times as
much.

> ⚠️ But careful: multi-level **storage** didn't lose, it just went somewhere else.
> The flash cells in SSDs and USB sticks today hold 4, 8, even 16 distinct voltage
> levels per cell (MLC/TLC/QLC). The difference: **storage is allowed to read
> slowly.** It can spend time on a cell, it can run error-correcting codes. A logic
> gate can't — it decides billions of times a second, and when it reads wrong there's
> no going back. That's why logic stayed binary; multiple levels live in storage that
> may read slowly, in flash.

## Who Enforces the Ban: The Transistor

In Lesson 01 we introduced the transistor as "the relay's grandchild": a switch
whose lever is pushed by electricity, with no moving parts. Now let's look inside.

The common type is the **MOSFET**. It has three terminals, and the middle one is
the critical one for us:

```
           GATE
            │
        ────┴────        ← an insulating layer
   ═════════════════     ← channel (opens/closes)
    │             │
  SOURCE        DRAIN
```

Apply voltage to the gate and a **channel** forms in the region beneath it, and
electricity flows between source and drain. Remove the voltage and the channel
closes. The job the coil does in a relay is done here by voltage alone — no
magnet, no movement.

> ⚠️ **A name collision — watch out.** In English, the MOSFET's control terminal is
> called the **gate**, and a logic gate is also called a **gate.** They are two
> completely different things. Whenever you read "gate" in this series, check which
> layer you're standing on: if we're talking transistors, it's the terminal; if
> we're talking logic, it's the component. (Turkish gets lucky here — the Turkish
> edition of this lesson uses two separate words, *geçit* for the terminal and
> *kapı* for the logic gate.)

> 💡 **If you remember "base and collector," you're not wrong — those belong to a
> different family.** A transistor isn't one single part; there are two big families
> and their terminals have different names:
>
> | family | terminals | where |
> |---|---|---|
> | **MOSFET** (field-effect) | gate · source · drain | every modern chip, this lesson |
> | **BJT** (bipolar junction) | base · collector · emitter | discrete circuits, analogue, old TTL logic |
>
> Both are transistors, both switch. This series cares about the MOSFET because
> CMOS is built out of it.

Now the most important detail, because the next two sections rest on it:

> 🔑 **The gate terminal is insulated — no current flows through it.** The gate
> doesn't touch the channel; there's a thin insulating layer in between. Which means
> holding a transistor open costs you almost nothing. The gate behaves like a small
> **capacitor**: *charging* it and *discharging* it takes energy, but holding it
> charged does not.

Hold on to that sentence: **keeping a state is almost free; energy is really spent
when CHANGING it.**

## CMOS: Why in Pairs

The MOSFET comes in two flavours, and they work as each other's opposite:

| type | conducts when |
|---|---|
| **n-type** | its gate is **high** |
| **p-type** | its gate is **low** |

Every modern chip uses these two in **pairs.** That's where the name comes from:
**CMOS** — *Complementary MOS*, "MOS that complete each other."

The simplest example is the NOT gate (the inverter):

```
              VDD (1)
               │
            [ p-type ]
               │
  input ───────┼─────── output
               │
            [ n-type ]
               │
              GND (0)
```

- **Input 0:** p conducts, n is cut off → the output is tied upward to `VDD` → `1`
- **Input 1:** n conducts, p is cut off → the output is tied downward to `GND` → `0`

While the input is stable, in both cases **one of the two is off.** So no
uninterrupted path from supply to ground opens. (`GND`: ground, 0 V.)

The result: **static current ≈ 0.**

> 🔑 This is why a chip with billions of transistors doesn't melt while sitting
> idle. The transistors are all there, connected to the supply, but there is no open
> path from supply to ground.

Still, it isn't zero: an off transistor doesn't cut current off completely either; a
very small **leakage current** seeps through it. Negligible in one transistor, but
added up over billions, a chip sitting still spends some energy too.

There's a second gain here you might not notice: the output is tied either to a
full `VDD` or a full `GND` — never to somewhere in between. **That is exactly the
signal restoration from the previous section.** The gate looks at a dirty input and
drags its output all the way to a rail.

## So Where Does the Heat Come From?

If the static current is zero, why does a processor get hot?

The answer: **at the moments of transition.** When a gate goes from `0` to `1`, two
things happen:

1. For a very brief instant both p and n partially conduct — a momentary
   short-circuit current passes from supply to ground
2. The wire on the output, and every gate terminal listening to it, behaves like a
   capacitor; all of them have to be charged or discharged again

Both happen **only at the moment of change.** A gate that's sitting still is left
with only that small leakage current.

We can compress all of this into one relation:

```
P ≈ C · V² · f
```

- **C** — capacitance: how many wires you're driving, and how long they are
- **V** — supply voltage
- **f** — how many times per second things change (clock frequency)

Those three letters explain the last twenty years of computing history:

- **Voltage is squared.** Drop `V` by 10% and power drops 19%. That's where you
  read off why undervolting works so well, and why manufacturers fight to shave
  voltage every generation.
- **Frequency is linear — but not free.** To stay stable at a higher frequency you
  usually have to raise the voltage too. Combine the two and power climbs faster
  than the square of frequency.
- **Why the GHz race ended.** In the early 2000s processors climbed to a higher
  clock every year; around 2005 they hit a wall. The wall was this relation. It's
  the reason the industry went **multi-core** instead of making one core faster.

## 🔬 Measure It on Your Own Chip

The claim is: **energy is spent flipping bits, not doing work.** You don't have to
take my word for it — you can look.

On Linux there's a file that counts the energy the processor has spent:

```bash
cat /sys/class/powercap/intel-rapl:0/energy_uj
```

> 📎 The filename says `intel`, but AMD processors use the same interface — don't be
> thrown by it.

This counter climbs continuously, in microjoules. Divide the difference between two
readings by the elapsed time and you get **watts**:

```bash
# while idle (the file is open only to root, hence sudo; the reason is below)
A=$(sudo cat /sys/class/powercap/intel-rapl:0/energy_uj); sleep 4
B=$(sudo cat /sys/class/powercap/intel-rapl:0/energy_uj)
awk -v a=$A -v b=$B 'BEGIN { printf "%.2f W\n", (b-a)/4000000 }'
```

(`awk` does the division because the shell's own arithmetic only knows whole
numbers: it would print `19` instead of `19.98`.)

Then keep every core busy for 12 seconds and take the same measurement **while the
load is running:**

```bash
for i in $(seq 1 $(nproc)); do (timeout 12 bash -c 'while :; do :; done' &); done
sleep 2     # let the load settle; then run the three lines above
```

On this machine (Ryzen 9 8940HX, 16 cores) the result:

| state | power | cpu0 frequency |
|---|---|---|
| idle | **19.98 W** | 1.85 GHz |
| all cores busy | **37.27 W** | 2.38 GHz |

The transistor count didn't change; it's the same chip. What mainly changed is **how
many gates change state per second:** the cores are busy and the frequency rose too
(1.85 → 2.38 GHz). The power nearly doubled.

> 📌 This measurement does not isolate switching. At idle most cores are in deep
> sleep and the chip cuts their supply; under load they wake up. The voltage rises
> with the frequency, and power grows with the square of the voltage. The increase is
> the sum of these three. But all three share one cause: gates are going to change
> state.

### Why `sudo`?

A `cat` without `sudo` will most likely give "Permission denied"; the file is
readable only by root. The reason is precisely this lesson's subject.

In 2020 a piece of research called **PLATYPUS** showed that by reading this power
counter often enough, you could infer what data the processor was working on at the
time — encryption keys included. The whole chain of reasoning is already built in
this lesson:

```
data changes → a different number of gates flip → different power is drawn → the counter shows the difference
```

The Linux kernel answered by closing the counter to ordinary users. So that
"Permission denied" isn't a glitch — it's **a defence.**

> 👾 **For the curious:** This leak has a name and a page of its own →
> [CWE-1300 — Physical side channel](../cwe/cwe_1300.md). The physics underneath the
> sentence you'll read there, "a gate spends energy when it changes state," is the
> physics we built in this lesson.
